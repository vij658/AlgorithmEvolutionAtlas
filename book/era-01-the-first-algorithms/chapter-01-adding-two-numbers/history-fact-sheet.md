# Chapter 1 history fact sheet

What I checked, where, what the source says, how sure we are, and what I decided **not** to claim. Companion to [the chapter 1 outline](outline.md) and [the design decisions](../../00-about-the-book/design-decisions.md).

**How to use this sheet.** The quotations were pulled with a page-fetching tool that summarizes each page, so they are *extracted* wording, usually verbatim but not guaranteed to be. **Re-read each source page before a quotation is printed in the book.** Labels: **documented** (a source says it directly), **conjecture** (the object is real; its purpose is a guess), **reconstructed** (my imagined scene; the words are invented), **myth** (a popular story the sources contradict).

## Section 1: marks and bunches

- **Lebombo bone.** [tally] "The Lebombo bone, dated between 44,200 and 43,000 years old, is a baboon's fibula with 29 distinct notches." The page hedges: "A number of anthropological artefacts have been conjectured to be tally sticks." Label: **conjecture** (as to purpose).
- **Dolní Věstonice wolf bone.** [tally] "Dated to the Aurignacian, approximately 30,000 years ago, the bone is marked with 55 marks which some believe to be tally marks." Label: **conjecture**.
- **Ishango bone.** [tally] "…dated to the Upper Palaeolithic era, around 18,000 to 20,000 BC. It has a series of possible tally marks carved in three columns." Label: **conjecture**.
- **Grouping in fives.** [marks] "Tally marks are typically clustered in groups of five for legibility." And: "The cluster size 5 has the advantages of (a) easy conversion into decimal for higher arithmetic operations and (b) avoiding error, as humans can far more easily correctly identify a cluster of 5 than one of 10." The page says nothing about when or where the practice began. Do not date it.
- **Calculus.** [etym] *calculus*: "reckoning, account," originally "pebble used as a reckoning counter," diminutive of *calx*. *Calculate* first used in English in the 1560s.
- **Egyptian numerals.** [egypt] "Adding numeral hieroglyphs is easy. One just adds the individual symbols, but replacing ten copies of a symbol by a single symbol of the next higher value." And: "To make up the number 276, for example, fifteen symbols were required: two 'hundred' symbols, seven 'ten' symbols, and six 'unit' symbols." The page does not name the pictures for the signs. Label: **documented**.

## Section 2: how many make a bunch

- **Uruk bookkeeping.** [proto] Uruk IV: "The Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative dates approximately this period from 3350 to 3200BC." Uruk III: "…using the 3200–3000BC range for this phase." "The vast majority of proto-cuneiform texts, approximately 85% of the total (about 2000 from the Uruk IV period and 3600 from Uruk III), are administrative, bookkeeping records." "There were thirteen numerical systems in total (…)". "Different products used different measurement systems, which could change with the context. In a single tablet the (Bisexagesimal System B) could be used for grain rations, (ŠE system Š) for barley, and (ŠE system Š") for emmer wheat." Label: **documented**.
- **Pounds, shillings, pence.** [lsd] "The abbreviation originates from the Latin currency denominations librae, solidi, and denarii." The ratios were "introduced and imposed across Western Europe by Carolingian Emperor Charlemagne." "Under this system, there were 12 denarii in a solidus and 20 solidi (or 240 denarii) in a libra." "King Offa of Mercia adopted the Frankish silver standard of librae, solidi, and denarii into Britain in the late 8th century." The system "was maintained in Britain until 1971." The page does not explain the silver-weight basis for the ratios; do not claim one.
- **Decimal Day.** [decimal] "Monday 15 February 1971." The pound was "divided into 20 shillings, each of 12 (old) pence, for a total of 240 pence." Afterwards the shilling was abolished and the pound divided into 100 new pence.

## Section 3: the board

- **Salamis tablet.** [salamis] "dating from around 300 BC"; "a marble counting board (an early counting device)"; "Pebbles (Latin: calculi) were placed at various locations and could be moved as calculations were performed"; "each counter represents one unit of a magnitude determined by position." **Do not give a discovery year:** the "Salamis Tablet" page says 1846, the "Counting board" page says 1899.
- **Counting board.** [board] "This simple counting board consists of a wooden board with lines drawn on it, with pebbles (Latin: calculi) used as counters."
- **The Exchequer table.** [exch] "The name Exchequer referred to the resemblance of the table to a chess board (French: échiquier) as it was covered by a black cloth bearing stripes of about the breadth of a human hand in a chequer-pattern." Counters "were placed representing various values". "The spaces represented pounds, shillings and pence." The "calculator" was "responsible for placing the counters on the counting table". The page also mentions "wooden tally sticks issued as a receipt for each deposit." Label: **documented**; any scene built on it is **reconstructed**.
- **Dialogus de Scaccario.** [dial] "written in the late 12th century by Richard FitzNeal"; "The date of authorship of the book is disputed" (before 1179, or 1181 or 1188). This page does not describe the table; the Exchequer page does. I could not read the Fordham translation (its robots.txt blocks fetching).
- **Exchequer tallies.** [tally] "Henry I introduced the tally stick system in medieval England in around 1100." "The split tally of the Exchequer remained in continuous use in England until 1826." In 1834 the tallies, "representing six centuries' worth of financial records," were ordered burned; "The resulting fire set the chimney ablaze and then spread until most of the building was destroyed." (The fetched text says "the building"; do not name Parliament without checking.)
- **Roman numerals.** [rome] "The Roman system was cumbersome and posed a serious obstacle to calculations." "Computation with the abacus was quite common and, to some extent, overcame the difficulties of calculation with pen and paper." The page's worked example: LXXXVI times XLI.

## Section 4: paper, zero, and the word

- **Babylon.** [babylon] "the Babylonians only had to learn two symbols to produce their base 60 positional system." "A much more serious problem was the fact that there was no zero to put into an empty position. The numbers sexagesimal numbers 1 and 1,0, namely 1 and 60 in decimals, had exactly the same representation." "later Babylonian civilisations did invent a symbol to indicate an empty place" (no date given on this page).
- **Placeholder, zero.** [zero] "By 300 BC, a punctuation symbol (two slanted wedges) was repurposed as a placeholder." (Also three hooks used by a scribe on a Kish tablet "dating to as early as 700 BC.") "Rules governing the use of zero appeared in Brahmagupta's Brahmasputha Siddhanta (7th century), which states the sum of zero with itself as zero, and incorrectly describes division by zero." Maya: "…zero became an integral part of Maya numerals, with a different, empty tortoise-like 'shell shape'… it is assumed not to have influenced Old World numeral systems." The Bakhshali manuscript (black-dot zero) has contested dating: radiocarbon on six folios gave different centuries; the Bodleian dates the manuscript to AD 799–1102. Do not state a date for it.
- **Aryabhata.** [han] "Around 500, the astronomer Aryabhata uses the word kha ('emptiness') to mark 'zero' in tabular arrangements of digits." Usable as a one-line aside.
- **al-Khwarizmi's arithmetic.** [alg1] "Al-Khwārizmī's second most influential work was on the subject of arithmetic, which survived in Latin translations but is lost in the original Arabic." Written "about 825". "Dixit Algorizmi ('Thus spake Al-Khwarizmi') is the starting phrase of a manuscript in the University of Cambridge library, which is generally referred to by its 1857 title Algoritmi de Numero Indorum. It is attributed to the Adelard of Bath." "These texts described algorithms on decimal numbers (Hindu–Arabic numerals) that could be carried out on a dust board." Do not claim that any particular teacher used a dust board.
- **Algorism.** [algorism] "Algorism is the technique of performing basic arithmetic by writing numbers in place value form and applying a set of memorized rules and facts to the digits." The word "comes from the name Al-Khwārizmī (c. 780–850)". "During the 17th century, the French form for the word – but not its meaning – was changed to algorithm, following the model of the word logarithm, this form alluding to the ancient Greek arithmos = number." "…it wasn't until the late 19th century that 'algorithm' took on the meaning that it has in modern English."
- **Word dates.** [algorithm] "The word 'algorism' appeared in English by circa 1225, meaning 'the use of place-value notation in calculations.'" "In the 15th century, under the influence of the Greek word ἀριθμός (arithmos, 'number'; cf. 'arithmetic'), the Latin word was altered to algorithmus." "By 1596, Thomas Hood employed the form 'algorithm' in English." *Disagreement to handle:* an earlier fetch of Merriam-Webster gave "first known use 1811" for *algorithm*; that conflicts with Hood 1596 unless it refers to a narrower sense. If a date is printed, cite the exact source and say what sense it covers.
- **Chaucer.** [algorithm] "Geoffrey Chaucer used a variant in the late 14th century to describe 'augrym stones'." [chaucer] Miller's Tale, line 3210: "His augrym stones layen faire apart," glossed "His counting stones (for his abacus) lie neatly apart." So the word that began as the name of the written method had come to name the pebbles.
- **Fibonacci.** [liber] "was a 1202 Latin work on arithmetic by Leonardo of Pisa, posthumously known as Fibonacci." "A revised version of Liber Abaci, dedicated to Michael Scot, appeared in 1228." Prologue (as translated on the page): "There from a marvelous instruction in the art of the nine Indian figures, the introduction and knowledge of the art pleased me so much above all else." and his father "had me in my youth brought to him, looking to find for me a useful and comfortable future" at Bugia. "The first section introduces the Hindu–Arabic numeral system, including its arithmetic and methods for converting between different representation systems." "The second section presents examples from commerce, such as conversions of currency and measurements, and calculations of profit and interest." "The spread of the Hindu-Arabic system, however, as Ore writes, was 'long-drawn-out'…" (An earlier fetch said his father held a post at the customs house in Bugia; re-confirm that before printing it.)
- **Adoption.** [han] "It was used in European mathematics from the 12th century, and entered common use from the 15th century to replace Roman numerals."
- **Florence, 1299.** [nothaft] The statute "barred its members from writing (or letting others write) down credits or debits in their account books 'by means of or in the letter of the abacus, but let him write it openly and in full by way of letters.'" "The reasons behind this prohibition are a matter of speculation. A concern that the Hindu-Arabic numerals, which existed in a variety of forms, might lend themselves to forgery or fraud has been one of the more popular explanations." The modern myth has three claims: "(i) the number zero was feared for being associated with the Devil; (ii) it was therefore outlawed by church or state (or both); (iii) owing to these prohibitions, zero and Hindu-Arabic numerals were for some time only used under the cover of secrecy, hence the etymology of the English noun 'cipher'." Verdict: "All three of these claims must be rejected. The first and third are false outright in the sense that no compelling evidence to support them has ever surfaced…" Labels: the statute is **documented**; the devil story is a **myth**. (I did not see the journal and year of the article; cite title, author and the Oxford archive.)
- **Reisch's woodcut.** [eastaway] "In 1503, a German monk by the name of Gregor Reisch wrote an educational encyclopaedia called Margarita Philosophica." "On the right is a despondent-looking Pythagoras using a counting board… On the left is a happy-looking Boethius, a scholar from ancient Rome, using our modern 'Indo Arabic' numerals." "from the expression on his face, it's clear that Boethius thinks he has won." "The illustrator has made at least a couple of mistakes!" and "each of the digits is one away from what it should be." Present as **one commentator's reading** of an allegory (Boethius lived long before the numerals reached Europe).
- **Adam Ries.** [ries] "Adam Ries (1492 or 1493 – 30 March 1559) was a German mathematician." 1518: "…describes calculating on the lines of a calculating board, a kind of abacus." 1522: "Besides calculating on the calculating board, he describes numerical calculations with Indian/Arabic digits." "nach Adam Riese" "is used when speaking about simple arithmetic." The title spellings differ between sources; do not print the German titles without checking.

## Section 5: the machine

- **Pascal.** [pascal] "Pascal was led to develop a calculator by the laborious arithmetical calculations required by his father's work as the supervisor of taxes in Rouen, France." "Blaise Pascal began to work on his calculator in 1642, when he was 18 years old." "The sautoir is the centerpiece of the Pascaline's carry mechanism." "During its free fall the sautoir behaves like an acrobat jumping from one trapeze to the next without the trapezes touching each other." "Pascal used gravity to arm the sautoirs." "…each wheel is independent of the other" and "a machine with 10,000 wheels would work as well as a machine with two wheels." Of Schickard's earlier machine: "The cumulative friction and inertia of all these wheels could '…potentially damage the machine if a carry needed to be propagated through the digits, for example like adding 1 to a number like 9,999'." Of Leibniz's stepped reckoner: "the operator had to check the result and manually correct the possible errors" after multiple carry transfers. Subtraction by nine's complement; "around twenty more machines" in the next decade; "Nine Pascal calculators presently exist."
- **Babbage.** [cla] "Charles Babbage recognized the performance penalty imposed by the ripple-carry used in his difference engine, and subsequently designed mechanisms for anticipating carriage for his never-built analytical engine." [menabrea] The list of drawings in the Lovelace translation (London: Richard and John E. Taylor, 1843, *Scientific Memoirs* vol. III) includes: "11. Plan of mechanism for carrying the tens (by anticipation), connected with long pinions." (The fetched text printed the original's date as "1812", evidently a typo; do not cite the French publication date without checking.) [smg] "Hoarding and anticipating carriages." Babbage, 7 November 1863 – 23 December 1863; a technical drawing, 53 cm × 72 cm, from the Babbage Papers (BAB/P/179).
- **1946 report.** [bgvn] §5.5: "In a parallel accumulator, the first step in an addition is to add each digit of the addend to the corresponding digit of the augend. The second step is to perform the carries, and this must be done in sequence since a carry may produce a carry." §5.5: "Clearly it is inefficient to allow 39 times as much time for the second step (performing the carries) as for the first step (adding the digits). Hence either the carries must be accelerated, or use must be made of the average number of carries or both." §5.6: "For our case n = 40 we have a_n ≤ log₂40 ~ 5.3, i.e. an average length of about 5 for the longest carry sequence."
- **Early completion.** [early] "On average, an n-bit asynchronous ripple carry adder will finish in O(log n) time." The page cites Cheng, Unger and Theobald, not Burks et al.
- **Carry-lookahead.** [cla] An addition of two digits "is said to generate if the addition will always carry, regardless of whether there is an input-carry" and "propagate if the addition will carry whenever there is an input carry." The carry recurrence: "a digit of addition carries precisely when either the addition generates or the next less significant bit carries and the addition propagates." "A standard 16-bit ripple-carry adder would take 16 × 2 − 1 = 31 gate delays" against 8 for carry-lookahead. "Konrad Zuse is thought to have implemented the first carry-lookahead adder in his 1930s binary mechanical computer, the Zuse Z1." "Two widely used implementations of the concept are the Kogge–Stone adder (KSA) and Brent–Kung adder (BKA)."
- **Kogge–Stone.** [ks] Kogge, Peter Michael; Stone, Harold S. (August 1973). "A Parallel Algorithm for the Efficient Solution of a General Class of Recurrence Equations." *IEEE Transactions on Computers*. C-22 (8): 786–793. "Most designs use log2 n stages, doubling the width of the merged spans at each stage." "…there is significant wiring congestion; in the second-last stage of a 64-bit adder… necessitating 32 horizontal wires across the adder."
- **Binary.** [binary] Leibniz, *Explication de l'Arithmétique Binaire* (published in 1703). [stibitz] "In November 1937 he completed a relay-based adder he later dubbed the 'Model K'", which "calculated using binary addition."
- **TPU.** [tpu] "It contains 256x256 MACs that can perform 8-bit multiply-and-adds on signed or unsigned integers." "The 16-bit products are collected in the 4 MiB of 32-bit Accumulators below the matrix unit." The paper does not explain why the accumulators are 32 bits; the carry-bound explanation in the chapter is mine and must be presented as such.

## Bridge to chapter 2

- **Rhind papyrus.** [rhind] "It dates to around 1550 BC", Second Intermediate Period; the colophon: "This book was copied in regnal year 33 … from an ancient copy made in the time of the King of Upper and Lower Egypt Nimaatre." "The scribe Ahmose writes this copy." (The page I fetched for the multiplication method did not describe doubling.)
- **Egyptian multiplication.** [egmult] "This method may be called mediation and duplation, where mediation means halving one number and duplation means doubling the other number." "Although in ancient Egypt the concept of base 2 did not exist, the algorithm is essentially the same algorithm as long multiplication after the multiplier and multiplicand are converted to binary."

## Claims I decided not to make (not confirmed by any page I read)

- That the wolf bone's marks are grouped in fives.
- That Shannon's 1937 thesis contained a binary adder.
- That Weinberger and Smith published carry-lookahead in 1958.
- Any discovery year for the Salamis tablet (the sources disagree).
- The original French publication date of Menabrea's *Sketch* (the fetched text was garbled).
- That any particular teacher used a dust board, or that al-Khwarizmi's "dust board" is the same as a pen-and-paper method.
- That Florence's 1299 statute was about "satanic" numerals, or that zero was outlawed (the sources say the opposite).
- Anything about Chinese counting rods or other independent positional systems beyond the Maya zero: not researched. The chapter should say plainly that it follows one thread, the one that leads to the word *algorithm*, and that other cultures solved the same pains in their own ways.
