I came to catalog how this species learned to compute. Before they had writing, they cut notches in bone. By the time they built libraries, they had written down procedures that work for *any* number. Those procedures were their first algorithms. Each entry below records one new development. For each I set down the date as best it can be known, the problem that forced it, what changed, and where to watch, read and check the evidence yourself.

Before numbers, there is matching: one notch for each animal, each day, each debt. You need no idea of "seventeen" to keep this record; you only need to be able to make one mark per thing. It is the first data structure: an append-only log.

Fourteen strokes in a row cannot be read at a glance; ten strokes swapped for one new sign can. Once the signs exist, adding is pooling them, and whenever ten of one sign pile up you exchange them for one of the next. The exchange is the carry, and it is older than place value.

Here the same wedge means one, sixty or three thousand six hundred, depending only on *where* it stands. Position does the work that new signs did before. With position comes a new trick: to divide, look up the reciprocal in a table and multiply. Division turns into a lookup, the first precomputed table I have found.

A student drew a square and its diagonals, wrote 30 on a side and two numbers along the diagonal. The first is √2 in base 60, 1;24,51,10, which is 1.41421296…, correct to about six decimal places. No one measures that finely; it had to be computed. *How* it was computed is still argued over, 3,800 years later.

These scribes had no times table. To multiply 41 by 59 they doubled 59 again and again (59, 118, 236, 472, 944, 1888) and added the rows whose multipliers make 41 = 32 + 8 + 1. They never named base 2, yet every multiplication split the multiplier into powers of two. Swap the addition for multiplication and you have the fast exponentiation that today's encryption runs on.

On these boards nobody writes a digit. Counters go in columns; adding is pushing more counters on, then settling: any column holding a full group is cleared and one counter goes to the next column. The procedure lives in the hands. Their word *calculus* is Latin for a small pebble, and the English *Exchequer* takes its name from a counting table that looked like a chessboard.

Earlier procedures computed a *value*. This one proves something about *any* two numbers and stops on its own. Take the smaller from the larger, again and again; when the two are equal, that is the greatest common measure. Later readers noticed that one division does many subtractions at once. It still runs, unchanged in spirit, inside every encryption library they use.

π cannot be written down exactly. Archimedes traps it instead. A polygon inside the circle is too short, a polygon outside is too long, and every doubling of the sides narrows the gap. After 6, 12, 24, 48 and 96 sides: 223/71 < π < 22/7. He does not give *an* answer; he gives an answer with a guaranteed error bound.

Testing each number for divisors repeats the same work again and again. The sieve turns the problem inside out: never ask "is *n* prime?" Instead, for each prime, cross out its multiples, and whatever survives is prime. It is the oldest algorithm I have found that trades memory (a whole table of numbers) for speed.

In the first era, procedures were invented in a few places and written on clay, papyrus and parchment. In this one they travel: from China and India to Baghdad, and from Baghdad to Pisa. Along the way they gain a sign for nothing, a name ("algorithm" comes from al-Khwarizmi), and the first infinite series.

The clerks here faced a knot: several unknown quantities tied together by several conditions, so no single condition gives any one unknown. They did not untie it with words. They laid it out. Each condition became a column of rods on the counting board. Then they multiplied one column by the head of another and subtracted, column against column, until the board was triangular and the last unknown fell out. Subtraction sometimes left less than nothing, so they gave such quantities rods of a different colour and rules of their own. My hosts later named this procedure after Gauss, who was born nearly two thousand years after it was written down.

The sky-watchers of Alexandria had a geometry problem they met a thousand times a night: given an angle at the centre of a circle, how long is the chord across it? Measuring every chord is impossible, so Ptolemy computed them, in a way I now recognise as an algorithm with a seed and a set of growth rules. The seed is a few chords known exactly from the triangle, square, pentagon, hexagon and decagon. The rules are halving an arc and taking sums and differences of two arcs. Between the entries, a third column tells the reader how much to add for each part of a step. With that, a 360-row table could be built by hand and used for centuries.

A short puzzle in a Chinese primer turned out to hold a whole method. Count some things by threes and two are left; by fives, three; by sevens, two. How many things? Master Sun did not search. He kept three ready-made numbers, 70, 21 and 15. Each is a multiple of the other two divisors that leaves remainder 1 for its own. Multiply each remainder by its number, add, and cast out 105. Eight centuries later Qin Jiushao, who said he learned the rule from the calendar-makers, made it work for any divisors. He found the magic multipliers by a chain of divisions that my hosts would recognise as Euclid's algorithm run backwards.

For more than a thousand years the Babylonians wrote place-value numbers without any mark for an empty place, and readers guessed from context. When a placeholder finally came, it was a separator, not a number: nobody added it or multiplied by it. In India the mark became a number. In 628 Brahmagupta wrote down rules for zero next to rules for "fortunes" and "debts", which my hosts now call positive and negative numbers. Two centuries later a temple grant at Gwalior wrote 270 and 50 with a small round 0, in a form any modern child could read. His rule that zero divided by zero is zero was later rejected, and my hosts still give that case no value at all.

The task is to find whole numbers with x² − Ny² = 1. My sources write it Nx² + 1 = y², with the letters swapped. Guessing is hopeless: for N = 61 the smallest answer is x = 1,766,319,049. Brahmagupta noticed that two near-misses can be multiplied into a new near-miss, so a near-miss that is off by 1, 2 or 4 can be turned into an exact answer. Later Indian mathematicians closed the loop. Choose a multiplier that keeps the error small, compose, divide out, and repeat. The cycle always ends, though Bhaskara II probably could not prove it: the proof came six centuries later, in Europe. Europeans met the same equation five hundred years after Bhaskara and named it after John Pell, by what their own historians call a mix-up.

In Baghdad I found the man whose name became the word for what I study. Two of his books matter. One teaches the Indian way of writing numbers: nine figures and a sign for an empty place, then rules for each operation, fractions and square roots, carried out step by step in writing. Latin copyists turned his name into "algorism", and later writers into "algorithm". The other book sorts every linear and quadratic problem into six standard forms and gives a fixed recipe for each. For the hardest forms the recipe is a picture of a square being completed. It has no symbols, only words, and still it is a procedure that works for any numbers. His own species disagrees about whether he was original. Nobody disputes that he was copied.

A substitution cipher hides each letter behind another sign, but it cannot hide how often each sign appears. A philosopher in Baghdad wrote the trick down. Take a long plain text in the same language, count its letters and rank them. Count the signs in the secret text and rank them too. Then pair the two lists. When a message is too short for the counts to settle, he says, look instead at which letters like to stand next to each other. This is the first procedure in my catalog that needs facts about a whole language, gathered in advance, as well as the message itself. The treatise came back to light in an Istanbul archive only in the late twentieth century, and its rediscovery has two different dates.

In Pisa the merchants still wrote amounts in Roman numerals, which are fine for recording a sum and miserable for working one out. The son of a customs official, who had learned the Indian figures in North Africa, wrote them a manual in fifteen chapters. It begins with nine figures and a zero. Then come written rules for multiplying, adding, subtracting and dividing, then fractions, then problem after problem on prices, barter, partnerships and money changing. In chapter 12, almost in passing, there is a puzzle about rabbits whose answer, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, …, my hosts now name after him. Sanskrit writers had met that sequence centuries earlier. His real export was the written algorithm itself, put into the hands of people who did sums for a living.

This official wrote his mathematics while in mourning, and he put two procedures on the counting board that the West would find again centuries later. The first finds a root of an equation of any degree one digit at a time. Each new digit shifts the whole polynomial, so the next digit is found the same way as the last, and no power is ever computed directly. The second, the Da Yan rule, "searches for unity": it finds the multiplier that leaves remainder 1, and with it a number that fits any set of remainders. Calendar makers had used the rule for centuries. Qin is the first I can find who wrote it out in full.

Archimedes' polygons cost a new square root for every doubling. In Kerala I found something new: a sum that never ends. One minus a third, plus a fifth, minus a seventh, and so on, gives a quarter of the circumference over the diameter. By itself this sum is hopeless, because a million terms buy only a few digits. So the Kerala astronomers stopped after a few terms and added one correction that estimates everything they left out. That correction is the clever part. It is the first time I have seen anyone compute with an infinite process and then deal honestly with the part they did not compute.

This calculator did something I had not seen before: he set the error he would accept *before* he began. Take a circle 600,000 times as wide as the Earth. Its computed circumference must be off by no more than the width of a horse's hair. From that target he worked out how far to go: start from a hexagon and keep doubling the sides until the polygon has 3·2^28 = 805,306,368 of them (27 doublings). He did every step in base 60, on a circle of radius 60, and gave the answer in both sexagesimal and decimal fractions. Archimedes' method was already some 1,700 years old. What was new was the accounting: a precision fixed before he started, and a computation planned to meet it.

In the second era, procedures travelled. In this one they are made to run without a thinking person: printed as tables so that nobody has to compute, built into wheels so that nobody has to carry, turned into rules that correct themselves, and at last written as instructions for a machine. Along the way, logic becomes arithmetic and arithmetic becomes binary.

For three thousand years the people I have followed solved equations of the second degree by a recipe. In Italy the third degree fell, in secret, in a contest, and in a quarrel over a broken oath. The recipe that came out of it worked, but it had a trap: for some cubics with three perfectly ordinary answers, the recipe insisted on taking the square root of a negative number. Rather than throw the recipe away, Bombelli wrote rules for these impossible quantities and calculated straight through them to the real answer.

Astronomers and navigators of this time spend their nights multiplying long numbers. A Scottish laird spent twenty years on a cure: a table in which every number has a partner, chosen so that adding the partners does the work of multiplying the numbers. A London professor rode for days to meet him, and between them they settled the form that every pocket calculator used until the 1970s. The partners themselves were found by a brutal routine: take the square root of ten, fifty-four times in a row, each time to thirty decimals.

On the counting boards of the first era (topic 6), the carry was a decision: ten counters become one in the next column, and a person had to notice. In this century three men put the carry into brass. Schickard's machine burned. Pascal's dropped a small weighted lever from one wheel to the next, so a carry could ripple through every column without straining the gears. Leibniz built a drum with nine teeth of different lengths, so that one turn of a crank added a whole number, and a sliding carriage made multiplication into repeated addition.

Two gamblers stop a game halfway. How should the money be split? Merchants' arithmetic books had offered answers for a century and a half, and they disagreed. In one summer of letters, two Frenchmen settled it, by two different routes. Fermat listed every way the game could have finished and counted. Pascal reasoned one round at a time from the end, filling in a table of fair shares from the finished games back to the stopped one. It is the first time I have seen a hard question answered by building a table of easier answers.

Qin Jiushao (Era 2, topic 9) dug out a root one digit at a time. Newton did something bolder: guess, then ask what small correction would make the guess right if the curve were a straight line. The correction is never exactly right, so do it again. With each round the number of correct digits roughly doubles. Newton wrote out a new polynomial at every step; Raphson saw that one could keep correcting the same equation; Simpson saw that the equation need not be a polynomial at all. The method carries Newton's name, though three people made it what it is.

The Egyptian scribes of the first era (topic 5) multiplied by doubling, and so, without saying it, wrote every number as a sum of powers of two. An Englishman wrote such numbers down with only two signs around 1604 and left them in a drawer. Leibniz, eighty years later, made the system public and said plainly that he was not recommending it for ordinary use. What he saw instead was that its arithmetic needs no memorised tables at all: every rule is "one and one make ten". That is exactly what a machine can do.

Seven bridges joined the islands and banks of a Prussian city, and its citizens asked whether a walk could cross each exactly once. Listing every route is hopeless. Euler threw away everything but which land touches which bridge, and counted: every time a walker enters a piece of land he must leave it, so the bridges at each piece pair up, except at the start and the end. Four pieces of land had an odd number of bridges, so no walk exists. He called the question "so banal". It is the first time I have seen a problem solved by deciding what to ignore.

Astronomers have more measurements than unknowns, and the measurements disagree. Which answer is right? None of them, exactly. A French geometer said: choose the answer that makes the sum of the squared errors as small as possible, because then the errors balance one another and no single bad measurement takes over. The arithmetic is old (the elimination of Era 2, topic 1); the new thing is a rule for what counts as best. A young German had already used it, he said, to find a planet that everyone else had lost.

An asteroid goes round and round, so its position is a periodic curve, and Gauss wanted the sines and cosines that fit twelve observations of Pallas. Done directly, every coefficient needs every observation: twelve times twelve products. Gauss noticed that twelve is three times four. Fit the four observations in each of three groups first, then combine the three small fits. The work shrinks, and for long series it shrinks enormously. He left it in Latin among his papers, where it waited for the electronic computer to make it indispensable.

Since Briggs (topic 2), the world has run on printed tables, and the tables are full of mistakes: wrong digits computed, right digits set wrongly in type. An English mathematician noticed that a polynomial's values can be produced with no multiplication at all. Keep its differences in a column, and every new value is a chain of additions. Additions are what wheels do well. So he designed a machine of columns that adds and, at the end, sets the answer straight into type, so that no human hand ever copies a number.

Babbage's second machine (designed in the 1830s, never built) was different in kind: a store of numbered columns, a mill that does arithmetic, and a train of punched cards, borrowed from the Jacquard loom, that says which operation to do on which columns. A translator's notes, longer than the paper they annotate, worked out what such a machine could do: repeat a group of cards (a loop), act on symbols as well as numbers, perhaps compose music. To show it, she laid out every step of a calculation of a Bernoulli number. One step has its division the wrong way round. It is still the first such plan I have found in print.

Since Aristotle, logic had been a list of valid argument forms, learned by heart. A self-taught teacher wrote logic as algebra: letters for classes of things, multiplication for "and", and one law that ordinary numbers do not obey, x times x equals x. Only two numbers satisfy it, nothing and everything, 0 and 1. Reasoning became calculation, done by rules a clerk could follow. Eighty years later a student noticed that a switch, too, is either open or closed, and the algebra of thought became the algebra of circuits.

The young republic counts its people every ten years, and the counting now takes nearly as long as the interval. Clerks made a tally mark in a small square for every person with every property, then counted the marks. An engineer put each person on a card, as holes. A press brings pins down on the card; where there is a hole, a pin dips into a cup of mercury, closes a circuit, and a dial moves on by one. The same cards can be counted again and again for new questions, and sorted into drawers by what is punched on them. Tally marks, the very first algorithm I recorded (Era 1, topic 1), have become electrical.
